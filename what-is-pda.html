A careful introduction

What is PDA?

PDA is a term used to describe a pattern in which everyday demands or expectations may create an intense need to protect autonomy and reduce pressure. It is not simply a child choosing to be difficult.

How a demand may unfold

  1. Demand or expectation
    A direct request, transition, internal need, or implied expectation.
  2. Perceived loss of autonomy or pressure
    The demand may begin to feel inescapable or controlling.
  3. Nervous-system threat response
    Capacity can narrow as urgency and distress rise.
  4. Protective response
    Avoidance, shutdown, negotiation, panic, or escalation may follow.

Demand sensitivity is more than “won’t”

A person may want to do something and still find that the expectation makes access to it harder. This can vary by day, context, relationship, uncertainty, sensory load, and overall capacity. These are explanatory possibilities—not a diagnosis of any individual.

Why compliance-first strategies may add pressure

Repeated prompting, rewards, consequences, or firmer insistence can make the loss of autonomy feel larger. For some people, collaboration, indirect language, genuine choices, more time, and fewer demands may reduce the sense of threat.

Characteristics, not a checklist

Families may notice strong negotiation, avoidance through conversation or distraction, sudden loss of capacity, a need for control, masking, or intense responses to apparently ordinary expectations. The same behaviors can have many causes; a list cannot establish a diagnosis.

Education and diagnosis

Understanding is not assessment

Educational language can help families notice patterns and ask better questions. It cannot determine whether someone has PDA, autism, anxiety, OCD, PANS/PANDAS, or any other condition. Diagnostic questions belong with appropriately qualified professionals who can consider the whole person and context.
